Gamma, in one picture

Black level sets where black sits, and contrast sets where white sits. Gamma decides everything in between: how quickly the picture climbs out of black, and so how dark or open the shadows and mid-tones look.

What gamma does

A video signal isn’t light: it’s a list of levels from black to white. Gamma is the curve the TV uses to turn those levels into light. The ends are fixed, so black stays black and white stays white, but the curve bends the middle.

A higher gamma bends it further down: shadows and mid-tones come out darker, and the picture looks richer and more contrasty. A lower gamma lifts them: more detail in dark areas, but a flatter, greyer look.

Which one to use

HD and 4K SDR video is made for a standard called BT.1886. On a TV with good blacks it behaves almost exactly like gamma 2.4, and it assumes a dim room, like a grading suite.

2.4 or BT.1886
A dark or dim room. Closest to what the colourist saw.
2.2
A bright room. Room light washes out shadows, so lifting them a little brings detail back.
Lower than 2.2, or higher than 2.4
Rarely right. Low values look washed out; high ones hide shadow detail.

If you watch in both, use 2.4 at night and 2.2 by day, or keep 2.4 and accept slightly darker shadows in daylight. Some TVs number gamma differently, as steps like −3 to +3 instead of 2.2 or 2.4. If yours does, the default is usually the right starting point.

Gamma isn’t black level

If dark scenes look murky, it’s tempting to lower gamma. Check black level first: if black is set too low, the darkest detail disappears whatever the gamma, and if it’s too high, black turns grey. Set black level with a pattern, then choose gamma for your room.

HDR doesn’t use it

HDR video describes actual brightness rather than relative levels, using a curve called PQ, or HLG for broadcasts. Many TVs grey out the gamma setting when HDR is playing. For HDR, the settings that matter are black level and tone mapping: see HDR10, HDR10+ and Dolby Vision.
