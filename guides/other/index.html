Brand guide

TV picture setup

These use the most common menu names. Your TV may use slightly different words; look for the closest match.

Watching through a streaming box or console? Set that up first: a mismatch there makes blacks grey or crushed whatever you do on the TV.

01

Get ready

  • Update your TV’s software first (usually Settings → System or Support → Software update). Updates can change picture processing or reset settings, so do it before you start.
  • Turn the lights down to how you normally watch.
  • Open your TV’s picture settings menu with the remote. You’ll use it throughout.
02

Quick settings

Do these first. They make the biggest difference.

  • Picture mode: Filmmaker, Movie or CinemaIf you have Filmmaker Mode, use it. Otherwise Movie, Cinema or Calibrated.
  • Motion smoothing: OffAlso called TruMotion, Auto Motion Plus, MotionFlow or Motion Interpolation.
  • Energy saving and light sensor: OffAlso called Eco, Power saving, Ambient light detection or Adaptive brightness.
  • Colour space: AutoAlso called Colour gamut. Avoid Native or Wide: they make ordinary shows look oversaturated.
  • Colour and Tint: leave at defaultTurn off any Colour enhancer, Live colour or Vivid colour option. The picture mode’s defaults are the most accurate.
  • Gamma: 2.4 in a dark room, 2.2 in a bright oneSometimes shown as BT.1886 (similar to 2.4). It sets how quickly the picture brightens out of black. If your TV has no Gamma setting, skip this.
  • Noise reduction: OffAlso called MPEG noise reduction, Smooth gradation or Noise filter. It smears fine detail in streaming shows. Low is fine for an old, grainy source.
Where are these settings?

Usually Settings → Picture, then Expert or Advanced settings. Make the changes on the HDMI input the Chromecast is plugged into; most TVs remember settings per input.

03

Fit to screen

Look at the edges of your TV. There’s a thin white border around the very edge, with arrows in each corner.

Can you see the white border on all four edges, with arrows touching the corners?

If not: change Picture size to the option that shows the whole pictureIt’s called Just Scan, Screen Fit, Fit to Screen, Full Pixel or Original, depending on the brand. It may be under Picture → Aspect ratio.

Uses the fit-to-screen test pattern. The guided setup shows it on your TV for you.

04

Brightness

This makes dark scenes look right: true black, with shadow detail still visible.

Use the setting called Brightness. On some TVs, Brightness changes the backlight (the whole screen gets lighter or dimmer but the boxes don’t change). If so, use Black level or Shadow detail instead.

Look inside the grey frames. When it’s right, box A is completely gone and box B is a very faint grey square.

Not changing? Your TV may call this setting Black level or Shadow detail. “Brightness” on some TVs only changes the backlight. If box B never appears however high you go, see “Does your TV have local dimming?” below.

Does your TV have local dimming?

Many LCD TVs dim parts of the backlight in dark areas (called Local dimming, Local contrast, Precision dimming or Full array dimming). OLED TVs don’t have it.

The grey frames around the boxes are there to keep the backlight on, like in a real dark scene. Judge the boxes inside the frames, not the black edges of the screen.

If box B still never appears, even after raising Brightness a lot, local dimming is hiding it. Set local dimming one level lower (for example from High to Medium) and try again. Don’t keep raising Brightness: that makes every dark scene grey.

Uses the brightness test pattern. The guided setup shows it on your TV for you.

05

Contrast

This makes bright scenes look right, without losing detail in clouds, snow or white shirts.

Use the setting called Contrast. Don’t confuse it with Dynamic contrast or Contrast enhancer, which should be off.

When it’s right, you can see all three boxes. A is easy, B is lighter, C is very faint.

If box C never appears, also check that Dynamic contrast and any “HDR effect” or “Peak brightness” boost are off.

Uses the contrast test pattern. The guided setup shows it on your TV for you.

06

Sharpness

Most TVs add fake outlines to make the picture look crisper. Walk up close to the TV: lower Sharpness until the black lines have no bright outlines beside them.

Use the setting called Sharpness. Also turn off Edge enhancement, Super resolution or Reality creation if you have them.

On some TVs (often Sony), the neutral point is in the middle of the range. Going below it softens the picture, so stop as soon as the outlines disappear.

Uses the sharpness test pattern. The guided setup shows it on your TV for you.

07

Colour temperature

Switch between the colour temperature options while watching the TV. The white and grey areas show the difference most clearly: cooler options look bluish, warmer ones look yellowish.

Usually Picture → Expert or Advanced settings → Colour temperature (sometimes Colour tone or White balance). Options are often Cool, Standard, Warm 1 and Warm 2; LG uses a scale up to Warm 50, and Sony uses Expert 1 and Expert 2.

Start with the warmest optionWarm 2 on most TVs, Warm 50 on LG, Expert 1 on Sony. Flick back and forth to the other options to see the difference on the white and greys, then come back to it.

Give your eyes 30 seconds to adjust. Do the white and grey areas still look clearly yellow or orange?

If they still look yellow: go one step coolerFor example Warm 1 instead of Warm 2. Then wait a moment and look again.

Uses the colour temperature test pattern. The guided setup shows it on your TV for you.

HDR optional

Movies and shows in HDR or Dolby Vision use separate picture settings on most TVs. This checks them in a few minutes.

Is HDR on?

Press the Info or Settings button while the pattern plays: the picture mode name usually shows “HDR” (for example HDR Filmmaker, HDR Movie or HDR Cinema).

Choose the HDR picture mode: Filmmaker, Movie or CinemaHDR has its own picture mode and settings. Turn off dynamic contrast and energy saving here too.

Not switching to HDR? If your Chromecast plugs into HDMI: turn on the TV’s enhanced format for that HDMI port (called HDMI UHD Color, Input Signal Plus or HDMI Enhanced Format, depending on brand). On the Chromecast, set Display & Sound → Dynamic range → match content.

HDR brightness

Same as before, but for HDR. Most TVs keep a separate Brightness setting for HDR.

Use Brightness (or Black level / Shadow detail) while HDR is playing. Changes here usually only affect HDR.

Highlights

Four bright boxes, from bright (A) to very bright (D). Most TVs can’t reach the brightest levels, so they compress them. This checks they do it gracefully.

Look for Dynamic Tone Mapping, HDR Tone Mapping, Dynamic HDR or HDR Brightness in the picture menu. Names vary a lot by brand.

Leave Contrast at the HDR mode’s default (often the maximum). Turning it down dims all HDR.

If some boxes look the same: turn on HDR tone mappingLook for Dynamic Tone Mapping, HDR Tone Mapping or Dynamic HDR. If it’s already on, try its other options. Then look at the boxes again.

Uses the HDR test patterns. The guided setup shows them on your TV for you.

Dolby Vision optional

Dolby Vision adjusts the picture to your TV scene by scene, so there’s no pattern to tune. A few settings still matter, and most TVs keep them separate from the HDR ones you just set.

Start a Dolby Vision film or show, then pause it on a normal sceneLook for the Dolby Vision label in Netflix, Disney+, Prime Video or Apple TV. The TV only shows its Dolby Vision settings while Dolby Vision is playing, and changes you make now only affect Dolby Vision.
Choose Dolby Vision Dark (or Filmmaker / Cinema if there’s no Dark)Dark is the most accurate. Bright and Vivid boost everything. IQ modes follow the room light, which is fine for daytime viewing in a bright room.
Turn off the extras and leave the rest at defaultTurn off dynamic contrast, colour enhancers, motion smoothing and energy saving here too. Leave Contrast, Colour and Gamma at this mode’s defaults: Dolby Vision is already fitting the picture to your TV.

Find a dark scene. Do black areas, like letterbox bars or a night sky, look grey or glowing?

If they look grey: press Brightness down by 1Small changes only. If dark details start to disappear, go back up by 1 and stop there.

Your devices and room optional

  • Your devices
  • HDMI black level: AutoFor anything plugged in by HDMI (game console, Blu-ray, streaming stick). Also called RGB range or HDMI Black Level. Set Auto on both the TV and the device. A mismatch crushes or washes out blacks.
  • Streaming device: match contentOn a Chromecast, Apple TV or Fire TV, turn on match dynamic range and match frame rate, so films play at their natural speed without judder.
  • Streaming device resolution: 4KIf a separate Chromecast or streaming box is plugged into a 4K TV, set its resolution to 4K or Auto (on a Chromecast with Google TV: Settings → Display & Sound → Resolution). At 1080p, the TV has to upscale everything you watch. TVs with Google TV or another smart system built in don’t need this.
  • Film motion: natural 24pIf your TV has Real Cinema, Cinematic Movement or a 24p setting, turn it on. It smooths camera pans without the “soap opera” look.
  • Game console: Game mode and HDR calibrationUse Game mode (and HGIG if offered) on the console’s input, then run the console’s own HDR calibration screen. PS5 and Xbox both have one in their display settings.
  • Copy settings to other inputsLook for “Apply to all inputs” or “Apply picture settings: All sources”. Otherwise, repeat the values from your summary on each input you use.
  • Streaming quality: HighIn Netflix, YouTube and other apps, set playback or data usage to High or Best.
  • Your room
  • Backlight: set for your roomBrighter for daytime, dimmer at night. If your TV lets you save two custom modes, keep a Day and a Night version with only the backlight different.
  • Reduce reflectionsClose blinds or move lamps that reflect in the screen. Glare lowers contrast more than any setting.
  • Bias lighting (optional)A dim, neutral-white light behind the TV (6500 K, about a tenth as bright as the screen) makes blacks look deeper and eases eye strain in a dark room.
  • SeatingEyes level with about the middle of the screen, sitting straight on. LCD colour and contrast fade when viewed from an angle.
